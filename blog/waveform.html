What is a Waveform? - FuncSynth Origins

I had a very simple question that has a fascinating answer, and I ended up building a piece of software to demonstrate what I had discovered. Actually, two pieces of software.

My simple question, was this:

What is a waveform? What does it represent?

When you look at a DAW, you see something like this.

This lumpy thing is called a waveform. It's a visualization of sound. Any sound that your computer can make can be visualized as a waveform.

But like... visualize it how? What is this thing actually?

I think I know the answer, but rather than just tell you, I want to take you through the observations I made that got me there.

Observations

First, let's start with the obvious stuff that you can intuit from interacting with literally any software that manipulates audio.

  1. The waveform is a horizontal strip of "stuff" the length of the "stuff" seems to correspond with the length of the audio. More time, longer strip.

  2. When you listen to the audio, you "move" to the right along the strip, likewise, if you start in the middle of the strip, you start in the middle of the audio.

So the horizontal axis of the waveform corresponds to time. You probably already knew that, but I'm just covering my bases.

What is the "stuff?" Let's make some observations about it.

  1. It seems the blue stuff is symmetrical... almost?

  2. When the audio gets louder, there's more blue stuff.

  3. When the audio gets quieter, the stuff gets smaller and gathers towards the center. When there's total silence, the stuff sits in the middle as an infinitely thin line.

It seems that the "stuff" is where our actual sound lives, let's zoom in on the "stuff" to see if we can learn more.

  1. If we zoom in, we see the "stuff" is actually just a long contiguous line.

  1. If we zoom in even more, we find that it's actually discrete points. Audacity won't let me select in between the points, so these points must be the atomic "unit" of the stuff. Let's call these units "samples"

  2. Each sample seems to be a distance from the middle point, Audacity shows these values to be "negative" and these values to be "positive" it looks like 1.0 is as high as they can go and -1 is as low as they can go.

  1. The samples tend to smoothly interpolate from one to the next, creating the illusion of a curve. If there's an abrupt change in sample you hear a pop or a click.

  2. The curve is usually not symmetrical. It seems different sounds (eg: a person making an "e" sound, a piano, a guitar) each create a unique repeating shape.

So the "stuff" correlates to the sound, but is it the sound itself? Like there's a lot of sounds that could exist in the world, surely you couldn't represent all of them with a bunch of dots like this... right?

With all this information I still hadn't quite put it together. But then I saw this gif and it all made sense.

This is a speaker playing a 1hz, this is not slowed down, this is what the speaker looks like. 1hz means it's playing a sound that looks like this: a sine wave that repeats once every second.

If this sound, produces this motion in the speaker, then this sound, must produce this motion in the speaker.

The Answer

A waveform represents a series of 1-dimensional points over time. Each point (aka: sample) can be any value from -1 to 1. These points dictate the position of a speaker cone, when the sample is at 1, the speaker is fully forward, when the sample is -1 it's fully backward (this part might be backward), when it's at 0, the cone is at its resting position.

When the speaker cone is moved in this way, it either pushes or pulls air molecules in the room which causes ripples in the air that hit your ear drum. Each push and pull of air pressure on the cone causes tiny pushes and pulls on your ear drum which triggers signals in your brain that you perceive as sound.

Once I understood what a waveform was, a lot of things started to make more sense. The popping sound in my game was caused by the speaker cone being extended out and then being forced to rapidly snap back to the zero position.

Now that I have this mental model for sound, a bunch of other stuff just... make sense now. So for the rest of this video I'd like to tell you all the things that blew my mind about sound.

How do Microphones work?

When I was a kid, I had a microphone with a cable like this. It's an aux cable that was supposed to go in the special "microphone" slot on my dad's computer. One day I went to plug it in and by mistake I plugged it into the headphone slot and something weird happened.

It started... to play audio. Not particularly well, but it seemed that this device was perfectly happy to pretend to be a speaker.

For a long time I just sorta filed that away in the back of my mind. But since this revelation I've thought about that a lot more.

A speaker is just a plastic cone with a magnet controlling it's position, the "sound" is just an artifact of the air pressure changes caused by moving the cone around really fast to very specific points. All of the stuff "around" a cone (the mesh, the case, etc) is there to protect the cone from getting damaged or dirty while also allowing sound to pass through the parts it's supposed to.

A microphone works in kind of the opposite way. If a speaker is "just" a cone, a microphone is "just" a diaphragm that moves when air pressure changes. If we record the position of the diaphragm over time as a series of samples, we can then feed it back into a speaker to reproduce that recording.

How does Noise Cancellation work?

Let me show you something.

Say we generate a sine wave at 200hz with an amplitude of 0.5, then we layer on top of it another sine wave at 200hz with the same amplitude.

The resulting sound is a sine wave of 200hz with an amplitude of 1. That makes sense, we're just adding them together.

If we then offset the second sine wave over slightly, we get this new shape. This new sine wave is the mathematical sum of these two samples. If this guy is 0.1 and this guy is 0.2, then the result is 0.3.

This can produce some weird sounding results. Even weirder if we offset it by one half of a cycle... It becomes utterly silent.

This doesn't just apply to sine waves, if I record my own voice and make a copy, flip it over (which sounds exactly the same by the way) and then layer them on top of each other, it's silent!

When I first encountered this I thought it was a bug. I thought it was this funny weird little quirk about how the computer handles sound.

That's technically true. But this quirk only happens because it's simulating reality.

If you played a sound and then played the reverse waveform of that sound and both sounds hit your ear at the exact same point they would cancel each other out and you wouldn't hear anything. Your ear drum would simply not vibrate.

I can't find a good visualization of this but imagine if two waves collided in the ocean that were both equal-but-opposite sizes, one was a peak, the other was a trough, as they collide you end up with perfect stillness. todo: make this as a 3D visualization

This isn't just theoretical either. If you've ever worn noise-cancelling headphones you've experienced this technology. They have microphones on the outside that listen to ambient noise, inverse it, and then emit it into your ears. If you're wearing noise cancelling headphones on a noisy airplane, you're getting blasted with anti-noise.
